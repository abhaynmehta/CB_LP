Open the Ads Manager of almost any growing D2C brand and you will find the same thing: twenty or thirty campaigns, each made for a good reason at the time, many with a few hundred rupees a day. A festive sale, a new flavour, a lookalike someone wanted to test, a retargeting set that was duplicated and never switched off.

Each one makes sense alone. Together they compete for the same people, split the budget into pieces too small to learn from and make the account impossible to read.

## The brand and the problem

[The Better Beings](/work/the-better-beings/) sells electrolyte drink mixes on its own Shopify store, to an audience that is about 90% on mobile.

![The Better Beings Shopify home page on desktop: three electrolyte sachets under the headline "Thirst doesn't need calories"](/work/show/the-better-beings/01.w700.webp)
*The Better Beings storefront after the rebuild.*

The Meta account had grown the usual way. Audiences overlapped across campaigns, so the brand was bidding against itself. Most ad sets had too few purchases a week to leave the learning phase. And because results were spread over so many lines, nobody could say which part of the spend was actually bringing new customers.

## The rebuild: three campaigns, three jobs

| Campaign | Job | Who it reaches |
|---|---|---|
| New customers | Find people who have never bought | Broad audiences, with past buyers excluded |
| Come back | Convert people who showed interest | Site visitors, product viewers, add-to-carts |
| Existing customers | Reorders, new flavours, bundles | Past buyers |

Every campaign has one job and one way to judge it. Creatives that win in one campaign can be moved to another, but audiences never overlap, so the brand no longer pays twice to reach the same person.

Inside each campaign, fewer ad sets with more budget each. That gives Meta enough purchases per ad set to optimise, which is the whole point: Meta's delivery system learns from conversions, and it cannot learn from three a week.

## What happened

| Measure | Change |
|---|---|
| Ad spend | 12% lower |
| Sales | Same |
| Average order value | up 14% |
| Store visitors | up 56% |

Same revenue on less money is the headline, but the other two numbers explain why. More people reached the store because the new-customer campaign was no longer starved by a dozen small retargeting sets. Order value rose because the existing-customer campaign could push bundles and multi-packs to people who already trusted the product.

The storefront mattered too. The store was rebuilt mobile first in the same project, with clearer product pages and a sticky add-to-cart, and WhatsApp automation took over abandoned checkouts and reorder reminders. Ads bring people in; the page and the follow-up decide whether they buy.

## How to know your account needs this

- More than five or six active campaigns on a budget of a few lakh a month.
- Ad sets that sit in "learning limited" week after week.
- The same audience (say, website visitors) targeted in more than one campaign.
- Nobody can answer "how much did new customers cost us last month?" in one line.

If two or more of these are true, consolidation is usually worth the short learning dip.

## How to do it without breaking sales

1. **Pause, do not delete.** Keep the old campaigns paused for reference and a quick rollback.
2. **Move the proven creatives.** The winners from the old account go into the new campaigns on day one.
3. **Exclude properly.** Past buyers out of the new-customer campaign; recent buyers out of the come-back campaign.
4. **Wait out the learning phase.** Judge the new structure after two weeks, not two days.
5. **Read it weekly against one number.** For new customers that is cost per first purchase, not overall ROAS, which existing customers inflate.
